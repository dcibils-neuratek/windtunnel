Wind Tunnel — guide

What this is

A wind tunnel that runs in your browser. The air is not an animation — it is solved, every frame, by a real lattice Boltzmann Navier–Stokes solver on a 3D grid of roughly 186,000 cells. Put a shape in the test section and the wake, the separation, the vortices and the forces are all computed from that flow rather than drawn.

You can use the nine built-in shapes or drop in your own .obj, .stl or .glb model. It measures drag, lift and side force, reports them as coefficients with confidence intervals, and exports a full run report as CSV.

What it is good for: understanding why a shape behaves as it does, and comparing shapes against each other. What it is not: a certification-grade CFD package — read the limitations before quoting a number.

Your first test

  1. Pick a body. Start with Sedan, already selected.
  2. Watch the flow develop. The smoke sweeps downstream and the wake forms behind the body. The coefficients read settling… until it has.
  3. Wait for the badge under the trace to stop saying averaging. When it reads converged, the number has stopped moving and the error bar is small.
  4. Note the Cd. Then click Box truck and compare. That difference is the thing this tool is genuinely good at.
  5. Turn on the Slice and set it to Pressure. Red on the nose is the air being stopped; blue behind is the low-pressure wake pulling backwards. The gap between them is the drag.
  6. Drag the body around the test section, or set an Angle of attack, and watch the numbers respond.

The controls

Test body — the nine built-in shapes, plus Load model… for your own. Uploaded models get orientation controls (Up axis, Nose axis, Rotate 90°, Flip 180°) in case the automatic guess picks wrong, and On the road to rest it on the floor instead of centring it.

Flow — Wind speed and Body length set the physical scale; they convert coefficients into newtons but do not change the flow pattern, which depends only on Reynolds number. The Reynolds knob lowers lattice viscosity: higher means a more turbulent, livelier wake. Rolling road + wheels makes the floor sweep past and the wheels spin, as on a real road.

Attitude — Angle of attack pitches the nose up, Yaw turns it into a crosswind.

Visualisation — three ways to see the air (below), plus the Slice plane, Body to hide the model and see the flow through it, and LES for the turbulence model.

Solver — Single core or Multi-core (needs the page served, not opened off disk), the grid resolution, and pause/reset. Press Benchmark & tune once: it times your machine and picks the best worker count.

Reading what you see

Smoke is a cloud of tracer particles coloured by speed — good for spotting fast, slow and trapped air at a glance. Ribbons are smoke-wand streaklines: the trail of everything ever released from one nozzle, which is exactly what a real smoke wand emits, and the thing that visibly rolls up into vortices. Set the count to a single wand and drag it around to probe one spot at a time, the way a technician does. Streamlines are thin curves tangent to the velocity field at one instant.

The Slice plane has a colour key beneath it that changes with the field:

  • Speed — navy is stopped air, yellow-green is the free stream (marked with a tick), red is about 1.6× wind speed. Blue here means slow, not low pressure.
  • Pressure — blue is suction, red is compression, black is ambient.
  • Vorticity — blue and red are opposite directions of spin.

Speed and pressure look like mirror images wherever the flow stays attached — that is just Bernoulli. Where they stop mirroring is the wake: air there is both slow and low pressure, because separated flow dissipates energy rather than trading it. That failure is precisely what pressure drag is.

Measuring properly

A number is only worth quoting once it has stopped moving. The badge under the trace tells you: settling (the wake is still forming, nothing is being averaged yet), averaging (collecting), drifting (still trending — wait), noisy (stable but the error bar is wide) and converged (mean stable, 95% interval under 3%).

Error bars come from the blocking method rather than σ/√N, because consecutive timesteps in a shedding wake are heavily correlated and the naive formula would flatter the result badly.

To record a run: press ● Start capture, let it gather, press ■ Stop, then ⤓ Export. The CSV holds every test condition, the results with confidence intervals in both coefficients and newtons, and the raw time series. Capture stops by itself if you change the body, angle or wind speed mid-run, since a window spanning a change of conditions would be meaningless.

How it works

The air is solved with the lattice Boltzmann method on a 3D D3Q19 lattice. Each cell carries 19 particle-distribution functions that stream to their neighbours and relax toward the Maxwell–Boltzmann equilibrium every step:

fq(x + cqΔt, t + Δt) = fq(x,t) − (1/τ)·[ fq − fqeq ]

Through the Chapman–Enskog expansion this recovers the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with kinematic viscosity ν = cs2(τ − ½), cs2 = ⅓. Mass and momentum are conserved exactly; pressure comes from the density field as p = ρcs2, so no separate Poisson solve is needed.

  • Body surface — halfway bounce-back, i.e. a true no-slip wall. The body is voxelised from the same analytic primitives that build the mesh, so the solver feels exactly the shape you see.
  • Forces — computed by the momentum-exchange method: every population that bounces off the surface deposits 2·cq·fq of momentum. Summed over the body this gives real drag, lift and side force, then Cd = 2F / (ρu²A).
  • Turbulence — a Smagorinsky sub-grid (LES) model adds eddy viscosity where the strain rate is large, which is what keeps the wake alive instead of blowing up.
  • Boundaries — velocity inlet, zero-gradient outlet, free-stream far field, and an optional no-slip road for the ground-vehicle cases.

Honest limitations

A real car at 100 km/h sits at Re ≈ 107. On a 96×44×44 grid in a browser we resolve Re ≈ 103–104 (both numbers are shown in the panel). The physics — separation, recirculation, vortex shedding, wake momentum deficit, pressure recovery — is genuinely right, and the solver reproduces the textbook Cd of a cube (1.18 vs 1.05) and a cylinder (1.13 vs 1.17) well, ranks the vehicles correctly (truck 1.39 > sedan 1.12), and gives a wing a lift curve of the right slope and sign (Cl = −0.26 / +0.01 / +0.42 at α = −8° / 0° / +8°, against ≈1.9 per radian from lifting-line theory at this aspect ratio). But the reference figures listed above are full-scale values, and Cd is a strong function of Reynolds number for anything rounded or streamlined. At our Reynolds number the boundary layer is proportionally enormous, so skin friction dominates and slippery shapes — the teardrop especially — read far higher than their full-scale numbers. That is not a bug; it is what a real tunnel measures at this Re too.

Compare shapes against each other, not against a wind-tunnel report. Tunnel blockage is held near 10% of the test-section area, which still inflates Cd somewhat. Smoke advection is time-scaled for visibility, so particle speed on screen is not the physical air speed.
